What This Note Captures

This note wraps the Socratic study thread on Markov chain Monte Carlo from Blitzstein and Hwang's Introduction to Probability. The focus was not on memorizing the algorithm, but on understanding why a Markov chain can help us sample from a distribution that is hard to sample from directly.

1. The Goal of MCMC

The goal is to get approximate samples from a target distribution when direct sampling is hard. Instead of trying to jump directly to a perfect independent sample, we build a Markov chain whose long-run visiting frequency matches the target distribution.

long-run time spent in state≈target probability of that state
Memory hook: MCMC turns direct sampling into a carefully designed random walk.

2. Easy Sampling vs Hard Sampling

Easy example: Bernoulli

For a Bernoulli random variable, one uniform random number and one comparison are enough.

ifU<poutput1;otherwise output0

Hard example: shape known, total unknown

A difficult target may be known only up to a proportionality constant.

p(x)∝exp(-x4+3x2)

The hard part is often not evaluating the shape at one point. The hard part is normalizing the whole distribution or inverting its CDF.

3. The Normalizing Constant Problem

If we know a nonnegative shape function g(x), the true probability density is:

p(x)=g(x)C

where the normalizing constant is:

C=∫g(x)dx

Direct sampling may require this constant or the CDF. MCMC can often avoid both because it only compares two points.

p(y)p(x)=g(y)/Cg(x)/C=g(y)g(x)
Key answer from the session: the unknown constant cancels in the ratio.

4. Metropolis-Hastings in One Line

The Metropolis-Hastings move is: propose a new state, compare its target weight with the current state, then either move or stay.

Start at x
Propose y
Compute ratio
Accept or reject
Repeat
acceptance=min(1,target(y)target(x))

When the proposed state has higher target weight, it is accepted for sure. When it has lower weight, it may still be accepted, which helps the chain avoid getting trapped too easily.

5. Concrete Treasure-Map Version

We used a simplified finite example: there are many places, and each place has a known local score w(i). The target probability is proportional to the score.

P(i)=w(i)∑kNw(k)

If there are 10,000,000 places, the score of the current or proposed place may be easy to check, but summing all scores may be expensive. MCMC avoids the full sum.

CurrentProposedRatioDecision
place 1, score 10place 101, score 3030 / 10 = 3accept for sure
place 101, score 30place 51, score 1515 / 30 = 0.5accept with probability 0.5

6. How the Next Place Is Chosen

The next candidate is chosen randomly by a proposal rule. The proposal rule is not the target distribution itself; it is just a way to explore possible moves.

Nearby proposal

From place i, propose i+random jump.

Example jumps

Choose randomly from -100, -50, -10, 10, 50, 100.

Accept/reject

The proposal suggests where to try; the ratio decides whether to move there.

Important: the randomness appears twice: random proposal, then random accept/reject.

7. Why Standard Normal Is Not the Main Problem

You asked whether the standard Normal distribution is hard to sample. In practice, usually no. Computers already have efficient algorithms for it, such as transformations from uniform random variables.

Simple in practice

Named common distributions often have built-in, tested sampling algorithms.

Hard in practice

Custom, high-dimensional, or only-proportional distributions may not have a direct sampler.

8. Subset Exploration and Poor Mixing

You noticed an important practical point: in a huge space, the chain may only visit a subset of all possible states. That is normal, but it must be the right kind of subset: the chain should spend more time in high-probability regions and still be able to move between important regions.

Good chain

Explores important regions and moves between them often enough.

Poor mixing

Gets stuck in one region, misses other important regions, and gives unreliable samples.

Session checkpoint: MCMC samples are approximate and correlated. The algorithm is powerful, but proposal design and mixing matter.

9. Socratic Questions We Answered

What is the MCMC goal?

Make a Markov chain whose long-run visiting frequencies match the target distribution.

Why can direct sampling be hard?

The full normalized distribution, CDF, or inverse CDF may be hard to compute.

Why can MCMC avoid the normalizing constant?

It uses ratios, so the unknown constant cancels.

How does it move?

Randomly propose a candidate, accept high-weight moves often, and sometimes accept lower-weight moves.
