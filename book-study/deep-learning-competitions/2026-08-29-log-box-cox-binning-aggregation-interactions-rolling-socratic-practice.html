1. Log Transform: Preserve Ratios, Compress Scale

The book recommends log transforms for strongly long-tailed numerical features. The useful intuition practiced here was that equal multiplicative changes become equal additive differences in log space.

Raw scale

100 → 200 gives a difference of 100.

1000 → 2000 gives a difference of 1000.

Log10 scale

2 → 2.301 gives a difference of about 0.301.

3 → 3.301 gives the same difference, about 0.301.

log(2x)−log(x)=log(2)
Meaning: doubling is represented as the same-sized change regardless of whether the original value was small or large.

2. Box-Cox: A Family of Power Transforms

The book lists Box-Cox alongside log transforms for skewed numerical features. In practice, Box-Cox can be viewed as a family of power transformations controlled by a parameter.

y={xλ−1λif λ ≠ 0log(x)if λ = 0

A standard clarification used in the practice is that classical Box-Cox is applied to strictly positive values. This was why a positive skewed feature such as 1, 2, 3, 4, 1000 was the straightforward candidate in the tiny test.

3. Binning: Represent Range Structure Directly

Binning turns a numerical feature into intervals when the target relationship is more naturally expressed by ranges than by exact numerical distance.

Income < 30k

high-risk bucket

30k–80k

medium-risk bucket

> 80k

low-risk bucket

Tiny-test conclusion: if risk itself is organized around ranges, explicit bins can represent that structure directly.

4. Decomposing Time Columns

The book suggests splitting timestamps into useful components such as year, month, day, day-of-week, hour, weekend indicators, and days-to-event.

A raw timestamp can hide recurring behavior. For example, an e-commerce pattern concentrated on Friday evenings becomes easier to expose with features such as:

day_of_week=Friday,hour=19

5. Aggregation Features: Compare a Row with Its Group

The book highlights grouped statistics such as mean, count, maximum, and standard deviation by user, store, item, or another meaningful entity.

UserAmountsUser mean
A20, 30, 250100
B200, 220210

If the goal is to detect a transaction that is unusual for that particular user, a useful engineered feature is:

current amount−user historical mean
Language clarification from the practice: “unusual for that particular user, not merely large globally” means judging the transaction relative to that user's own normal behavior, rather than comparing only with the entire population.

6. Cross / Interaction Features

Two individually ordinary features can become highly informative when combined. The book gives interaction-style examples such as city × occupation.

The practice example was fraud risk that becomes high mainly when a transaction is both international and made at night:

international_AND_night=is_international×is_night

Tree models can learn some interactions internally; explicit crossed features can still make a useful relationship easier to expose, especially for models that do not discover it as naturally.

7. Rolling-Window Statistics

For time-series features, the book suggests rolling statistics such as 7-day or 30-day means. The key constraint is that prediction-time features must not use future observations.

For daily sales 10, 12, 11, 13, 50, a 3-day mean immediately before day 5 is:

12+11+133=12
Tiny-test conclusion: when predicting tomorrow, the mean of the previous 7 days is valid; any rolling feature that includes tomorrow or later days leaks future information.
