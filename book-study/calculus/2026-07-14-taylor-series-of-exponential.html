Deriving the Taylor Series of ex

Study Wrap-Up

We derived the Maclaurin series of the exponential function Socratically. The central idea was not to memorize the formula, but to construct the only polynomial series whose value, slope, curvature, and every higher derivative agree with the exponential function at zero.

1. Begin With Unknown Coefficients

Assume that the exponential function can be represented near zero by a power series:

P(x)=a0+a1x+a2x2+a3x3+⋯
Goal: choose the coefficients so that this series and ex have identical derivatives at the expansion point x=0.

2. Match Value, Slope, and Curvature

Value

P(0)=a0=e0=1

Therefore a0=1.

First derivative

P′(0)=a1=1

Every derivative of the exponential function is still the exponential function.

Second derivative

P″(0)=2a2=1

Thus a2=12!.

Third derivative

P(3)(0)=3!a3=1

Thus a3=13!.

3. Why the Factorial Appears

Differentiating the term anxn exactly n times produces:

dndxn(anxn)=n!an

But the nth derivative of ex at zero is always one. Therefore:

n!an=1⇒an=1n!
Meaning: the factorial is a correction factor. Without it, repeated differentiation would make the higher derivatives too large.

4. The Result

ex=1+x+x22!+x33!+⋯+xnn!+⋯

Each additional term makes the polynomial agree with one more derivative of the exponential function at zero.

5. Why Coefficient One Would Fail

If we tried 1+x+x2+x3+⋯, its derivatives at zero would begin:

For ex

1, 1, 1, 1, ...

For the uncorrected series

1, 1, 2, 6, 24, ...

The equation would fail from the second derivative onward. Dividing by factorials makes every derivative at zero equal to one.

6. What We Have and Have Not Proved

Matching derivatives determines the only possible coefficients for a power-series representation. A complete proof also needs Taylor's remainder formula to show that the approximation error approaches zero as more terms are included.

Rn(x)=ecxn+1(n+1)!

For fixed x, the factorial in the denominator eventually dominates the power of x, so the remainder tends to zero.
