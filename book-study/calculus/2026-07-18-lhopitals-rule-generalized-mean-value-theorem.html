How L'Hôpital's Rule Comes from the Generalized Mean Value Theorem

Study Wrap-Up

We did not treat L'Hôpital's rule as a formula to memorize. We traced it backward to Cauchy's generalized Mean Value Theorem, and then traced that theorem backward to Rolle's theorem by building an auxiliary function whose endpoint values agree.

1. The Problem: Why Does Differentiating Top and Bottom Work?

Suppose both functions approach zero at the same point:

f(a)=0,g(a)=0

Then the quotient has the indeterminate form 00. L'Hôpital's rule says that, under suitable conditions, the original quotient and the quotient of derivatives have the same limit:

limx→af(x)g(x)=limx→af′(x)g′(x)

The question is not merely how to use this rule, but why a ratio of function values can be replaced by a ratio of derivatives.

2. First Distinction: Derivative Definition vs. Mean Value Theorem

Derivative at one point

f′(c)=limx→cf(x)−f(c)x−c

This is a limit of secant slopes as a moving point approaches c.

Mean Value Theorem

f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−a

This says that somewhere inside the interval, an instantaneous slope equals the average slope over the whole interval.

Vocabulary question: a chord is a line segment joining two points on a curve; in Chinese, 弦. When discussing the entire line and its slope, secant line is more precise; in Chinese, 割线.

3. From Average Change in x to Average Change in g

The ordinary Mean Value Theorem compares a change in f with a change in x. To generalize it, compare the change in f with the change in another function g:

ΔfΔg=f(b)−f(a)g(b)−g(a)

Cauchy's generalized Mean Value Theorem states that there is some point c∈(a,b) such that

f(b)−f(a)g(b)−g(a)=f′(c)g′(c)

If g(x)=x, then g(b)−g(a)=b−a and g′(c)=1, so the generalized theorem reduces to the ordinary one.

4. Why the Generalized Theorem Is True

We manufacture an auxiliary function whose values at the two endpoints are equal:

H(t)=[f(b)−f(a)][g(t)−g(a)]−[g(b)−g(a)][f(t)−f(a)]
1

Endpoints

Substitution gives H(a)=0 and H(b)=0.

2

Rolle's theorem

Therefore some c∈(a,b) satisfies H′(c)=0.

3

Rearrange

Differentiating and rearranging produces the generalized Mean Value Theorem.

H′(t)=[f(b)−f(a)]g′(t)−[g(b)−g(a)]f′(t)

At t=c, set this equal to zero and move one term across:

[f(b)−f(a)]g′(c)=[g(b)−g(a)]f′(c)

Dividing by the nonzero factors yields the desired ratio equality.

5. The Final Bridge to L'Hôpital's Rule

Now choose the second endpoint to be the moving point x. Because f(a)=g(a)=0, the generalized theorem becomes

f(x)g(x)=f′(c)g′(c)

Here c lies between a and x. Therefore

|c−a|≤|x−a|

As x→a, the right side approaches zero, so c→a. If the derivative ratio has limit L, then the ratio at the intermediate point also approaches L. This proves the central idea of L'Hôpital's rule for the 00 case.

Conceptual summary: the generalized Mean Value Theorem converts a ratio of total changes into a ratio of instantaneous changes at a nearby point. The interval then collapses, forcing that nearby point toward the limit point.

6. The Questions That Unlocked the Proof

The questions were not interruptions; they exposed the exact distinctions needed for the derivation.

“What does chord mean?”

Chord, secant, and tangent

Chord = 弦, the segment joining two curve points. Secant line = 割线, the whole line through them. Its slope is the average rate of change.

“Is the equation the limit as x approaches c?”

Two different equations

The limit formula defines f′(c). The Mean Value Theorem states that this derivative equals an endpoint secant slope for some interior c.

“Which side is the average slope?”

The endpoint quotient

The right side f(b)−f(a)b−a is the average slope over the interval.

“Average change in f per change in g?”

It is Δf / Δg

This simple answer is the conceptual leap from the ordinary Mean Value Theorem to Cauchy's generalized form.

“Why build that complicated H?”

To activate Rolle's theorem

The coefficients are chosen so that H(a)=H(b)=0. Rolle then guarantees an interior zero derivative.

“What are H(a) and H(b)?”

Both are zero

Checking the endpoints made the purpose of the auxiliary function concrete instead of mysterious.

“After differentiating H, what happens?”

The desired equality appears

Setting H′(c)=0 and rearranging produces the ratio of changes equals ratio of derivatives.

“As x approaches a, what happens to c?”

c also approaches a

Because c stays between the two endpoints, the collapsing interval squeezes it toward a.

A useful correction: “f(a)/g(b)”

Keep the moving endpoint

When f(a)=g(a)=0, the change ratio simplifies to f(b)g(b), not f(a)g(b).

8. Conditions and Cautions

Do not differentiate every quotient automatically. L'Hôpital's rule applies to indeterminate forms such as 00 and, in its other standard form, ∞∞. It does not apply merely because a quotient contains complicated functions.

For the finite 00 case discussed here, the functions must satisfy the continuity and differentiability conditions required by the generalized Mean Value Theorem, the denominator derivative must not vanish where the quotient is used, and the derivative quotient must have the relevant limit.

Repeated use is legitimate only when the new quotient remains indeterminate and the rule's conditions continue to hold.

Final Takeaway

L'Hôpital's rule is not magic cancellation. It is a consequence of an intermediate-point theorem:

ratio of total changes=ratio of derivatives at some nearby c

Then the interval shrinks, c→a, and the derivative ratio controls the original quotient.
